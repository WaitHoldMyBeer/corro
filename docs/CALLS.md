# Calls: speech recognition and recording consent

Product framing, not legal advice. Written 2026-10-02. Sources are primary where I could fetch them (statute text, MDN); everything else is marked. A lawyer for the firm decides what is permitted in a real engagement.

## (a) What the browser's built-in speech recognition does with audio

- MDN, Web Speech API: "By default, using speech recognition on a web page involves a server-based recognition engine. Your audio is sent to a web service for recognition processing, so it won't work offline." (https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Speech_API/Using_the_Web_Speech_API)
- MDN, `SpeechRecognition`: "On some browsers, like Chrome, using Speech Recognition on a web page involves a server-based recognition engine." (https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechRecognition)
- So in Chrome the default needs a network and sends the microphone audio to a third-party service. Who runs that service and what it retains: not stated by MDN. A secondary source says it is Google's servers (https://www.assemblyai.com/blog/speech-recognition-javascript-web-speech-api); I did not find Google's own page on it, so treat retention and handling as **unverified**.
- On-device option: MDN documents a `processLocally` property ("This ensures that neither the audio nor the transcribed speech are sent to a third-party service for processing") plus `SpeechRecognition.available()` and `install()` for language packs. Which Chrome versions support it: a secondary source says Chrome 139 shipped an on-device mode (https://medium.com/@roman_fedyskyi/on-device-speech-uis-in-chrome-139-4b9f0397b9c9), **unverified** against Chrome's own documentation. If used, test on the demo machine: set `processLocally = true`, check `available()` first, and fall back to hiding the button.
- Microphone permission and secure-context requirements: not covered by the MDN pages I read. `http://127.0.0.1` is normally treated as secure for browser APIs (**unverified** here); test it.

## (b) Consent to record or transcribe a telephone call, general terms

- **Federal baseline, one-party consent.** 18 U.S.C. 2511(2)(d): it is not unlawful for a person not acting under color of law to intercept a wire, oral or electronic communication "where such person is a party to the communication or where one of the parties to the communication has given prior consent", unless done to commit a criminal or tortious act. (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2511)
- **California, all-party consent for confidential communications.** Penal Code 632(a) reaches a person who "intentionally and without the consent of all parties to a confidential communication, uses an electronic amplifying or recording device to eavesdrop upon or record" one; "confidential communication" is any communication "carried on in circumstances as may reasonably indicate that any party ... desires it to be confined to the parties thereto" (632(c)). Penalty text on the page: a fine up to $2,500 per violation or jail up to one year or state prison, or both, with a higher fine on a subsequent offence. (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=632.)
- **California, cellular and cordless calls.** Penal Code 632.7(a) covers anyone who "without the consent of all of the parties to a communication, intercepts or receives and intentionally records" a communication involving a cellular or cordless telephone, with the same penalty range. (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=632.7.) Case law and civil exposure beyond the criminal penalty: described in law-firm and secondary pages I did not verify; do not rely on them.
- **Other states.** A group of states require all-party consent; a list that includes California, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, Washington and others appears in secondary sources, which disagree on the count (https://www.recordinglaw.com/federal-wiretap-act-ecpa/). **Unverified** per state; calls across state lines have no clean answer in those sources.
- **Transcription is recording for this purpose** in the sense that a transcript of another party's speech requires capturing their audio. Whether a live, non-stored transcript counts as "recording" under a given statute: **unverified**; do not rely on a distinction.
- Lawyer-conduct rules on covert recording (ABA Model Rule 8.4(c) on dishonesty): I could not fetch the ABA page, so **unverified**; state rules and ethics opinions differ.
- Practical note: stating at the start of a call that it is being recorded or transcribed, and getting a yes, is the ordinary way to obtain consent; the product can show a reminder but cannot establish consent.

## (c) Product position that follows

1. **Typed call notes are the real path.** The lawyer types or pastes their own notes of what the provider's office said. That is the lawyer's own record, needs no one's consent to create, and runs through exactly the same checker code as Draft.
2. **The microphone is demo-only.** It feeds the browser's speech recognition into the same box, hears only the microphone on the demo machine (the lawyer's own voice, not a phone line unless deliberately routed), and is labelled as such in the UI.
3. If used with `processLocally = true` the audio stays on the machine; otherwise the UI must say audio goes to the browser vendor's service, per MDN. Show which mode is active.
4. The product never records or transcribes a call between others, never joins a call, and has no telephony integration. Anything a provider's office says is entered by the lawyer, or arrives as a message the provider sent to the firm.
5. The checker is a tool for the lawyer: it tests the lawyer's drafts, notes and the messages that arrive against the file. It is not a tool for examining a witness and the pitch should not describe it that way.
6. In the README and the judges' notes: "call mode is demo-only; consent rules for recording vary by state (all-party in California for confidential communications); a deployment would need the firm's own policy." No compliance claim.
